# Bloom formative playtest

## Purpose

Test whether Bloom is discoverable, comfortable, forgiving, and understood as open-ended play. This is not a test of whether Bloom teaches or measures developmental mastery.

Use four caregiver–child pairs, ideally two children aged 18–24 months and two aged 25–36 months. Treat every result as formative observation, not generalizable research.

## Before each session

- Obtain caregiver consent and explain that the child can stop at any time. There is nothing to finish.
- Use a session code only. Do not record names, contact details, photos, audio, or video.
- Disable device notifications and choose a comfortable screen brightness and volume.
- Protect the rest of the device with [Guided Access on iPhone or iPad](https://support.apple.com/en-us/111795), [screen pinning on Android](https://support.google.com/android/answer/9455138), or a dedicated non-sensitive test device. Confirm that leaving the game requires the caregiver's authentication.
- Load Bloom successfully, then disconnect the test device from the network.
- Keep the caregiver nearby. They retain control of the device and can stop immediately.
- Start with sound on and the device in its ordinary orientation unless comfort requires otherwise.

## Session

Allow up to eight minutes of free exploration, ending sooner whenever the child disengages.

1. Present Bloom without instruction.
2. If the child has not touched it after about 30 seconds, offer once: “You can touch it if you want.”
3. Do not coach, correct, praise performance, demonstrate a goal, or coax continued play.
4. Observe ordinary, missed, repeated, palm, simultaneous, and dragging touches as they occur naturally. Note whether the child touches an existing flower or places flowers near one another. Do not manufacture stress.
5. If a bouquet or flowering tree appears naturally, do not explain it. Observe whether the child pauses, touches it, or later attempts another nearby trio.
6. If the child remains comfortable, the caregiver may offer sound-off once. Do not force a comparison.
7. End when the child turns away, leaves, pushes the device away, signals “done,” or ignores it for roughly 20–30 seconds.

Optionally make Bloom available once more 24–72 hours later for no more than three minutes. Say only “Bloom is here.” Record voluntary interest; do not remind, reward, or persuade.

## Assent and stop rules

Approaching, reaching, or tapping is momentary assent, not continuing permission. Stop immediately for crying, escalating agitation, repeated startle, covering ears, squinting or averting, pushing the device away, unsafe device handling, caregiver concern, or any other indication of discomfort. Do not restart that session to obtain a better result.

After play, ask the caregiver:

- “What did you think Bloom was doing?”
- “What, if anything, seemed enjoyable?”
- “Did anything concern you about the sound, motion, brightness, or touch response?”
- “Would you offer it again, and under what conditions?”

Do not ask whether Bloom taught colors or another concept.

## Decision thresholds

Proceed to a distinct second-concept experiment only if:

- at least three of four children begin within 60 seconds, with no more than one neutral invitation;
- all four receive understandable visible feedback from ordinary and imprecise touches, without repeated apparent failure;
- revisiting a flower and creating a nearby relationship never appear to be dead or wrong actions;
- no child shows repeated distress and every child can disengage naturally;
- at least three caregivers describe Bloom as exploration or play without assuming guaranteed learning;
- at least two children show voluntary interest at the optional later revisit.

Hold and revise Bloom if any session reveals severe sensory distress, a crash, navigation away, a network dependency, resisted disengagement, or repeated apparent failure. Also hold if discoverability, comfort, or caregiver-understanding thresholds are missed. Change the implicated variable and repeat the same small protocol.

If the thresholds pass, test a game built around a distinct concept such as matching or relative size. Do not extract a shared framework until a second game demonstrates what is genuinely shared.
